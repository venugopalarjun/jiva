The problem I still haven’t solved

I’ve written a lot about getting attribution right. The hardest version of it is one I’ve spent more time on than almost anything — and haven’t cracked.

By Arjun Venugopal · 24 November 2026 · 5 min read
Why I'm writing these. I'm a product manager, not an engineer, and I'm building a fully-local Mac app called Jiva entirely with AI. The category I'm building in is owned by companies worth a billion dollars and more. So the real question behind these posts is a simple one: can one person who can't write code build something that stands next to what those teams ship? Note that I'm not claiming it will. I'm documenting the attempt — the parts that work and the parts that don't — because that's the only honest way to find out.

I have written a lot about getting attribution right — echo cancellation, over-split repair, refusing to guess a name. I want to be honest that the hardest version of this problem is one I have not fully solved, and have spent more time on than almost anything else in the project.

The unsolved version is keeping every voice under one stable label through a whole messy meeting — five people, changing mic distance, someone on bad network, AirPods switching, illness, people talking over each other — without being told in advance how many people are in the room. Get that perfectly and everything downstream gets easier. I have tried a long list of approaches: different clustering methods, techniques borrowed from research systems, various repairs and constraints. Most of them I tested and rejected, because on honest evaluation they did not actually beat what I already had, or they fixed one condition and broke another. Note that the primary lesson of this whole stretch is humility — this is a genuine frontier problem, and the bottleneck has turned out to be not cleverness but labelled evaluation data: being able to measure, on real meetings, whether a change is actually an improvement.

So the current strategy is not a magic algorithm. It is to build a trustworthy way to measure, certify what actually works on real audio, and lean on a one-tap correction so that when the machine is unsure, you fix it and it learns.

The honest lesson, for a build-in-public log, is that not every problem gets a triumphant fix. Some you make steadily better while admitting they are not done. Writing only about the solved ones would make this a highlight reel. The speaker problem is the one I most want you to know is still hard.
